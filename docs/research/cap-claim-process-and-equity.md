# CAP Claims: Process, Equality, and Unequal Effects

## Purpose

This document explains what a claim under the European Union's Common Agricultural
Policy (CAP) is, how an area-based claim generally moves from application to payment,
and how CAP can both reduce and create inequality:

- between farmers within one country;
- between EU Member States;
- between EU and non-EU farmers; and
- between people who can and cannot navigate the administrative and digital process.

CAP is not a single identical subsidy paid in the same way everywhere. EU legislation
sets common objectives, financing rules, control requirements, and intervention types,
but each Member State implements an approved **CAP Strategic Plan**. Exact schemes,
rates, evidence requirements, deadlines, and appeal procedures therefore vary by
country and year.

## 1. What is a CAP claim?

A CAP claim is an application by a farmer or other eligible beneficiary for support
under one or more interventions in a Member State's CAP Strategic Plan.

For an area-based claim, the applicant normally declares:

- their identity and status as an eligible beneficiary;
- the agricultural parcels they manage;
- the crop, land use, or agricultural activity on each parcel;
- the intervention being claimed, such as basic income support or an eco-scheme;
- compliance with applicable eligibility and conditionality requirements; and
- supporting evidence when a condition cannot be confirmed from existing records or
  satellite observations.

One application may contain many parcel-level claims and may combine several support
interventions. A claim is therefore not simply a request for money: it is a structured
declaration that specific land, activities, and practices meet specific rules.

## 2. The claim process

The detailed workflow differs by Member State, but the common pattern is:

```text
Strategic Plan and annual scheme rules
                  |
                  v
Pre-filled geospatial application
                  |
                  v
Farmer checks parcels, selects interventions, and submits declarations
                  |
                  v
Automated administrative and cross-checks in IACS
                  |
                  v
Area Monitoring System observes monitorable conditions
                  |
          +-------+--------+
          |                |
       Consistent       Possible discrepancy
          |                |
          |        Farmer notified or evidence requested
          |                |
          |        Amendment, photo, document, or inspection
          |                |
          +-------+--------+
                  |
                  v
Eligibility and payment calculation
                  |
                  v
Paying-agency decision and payment
                  |
                  v
Complaint or appeal where available under national procedure
                  |
                  v
National, Commission, and European Court of Auditors assurance
```

### Step 1: Rules and interventions are defined

EU Regulations establish the legal framework. Each Member State converts that
framework into an approved CAP Strategic Plan and more detailed national scheme rules.
The plan determines which interventions are offered, their budgets, target groups,
conditions, and planned unit amounts.

### Step 2: The farmer prepares a geospatial application

Area- and animal-based interventions are administered through the **Integrated
Administration and Control System (IACS)**. For land claims, the application is linked
to a digital parcel map. Member States use a **Land Parcel Identification System
(LPIS)** and a **Geo-Spatial Application System (GSA)** to identify land and record the
area claimed.

Applications may be pre-filled with information already held by the administration.
The farmer remains responsible for checking the declaration and correcting outdated
boundaries, land use, or other claim data.

### Step 3: The claim is submitted

The farmer selects the relevant interventions and declares the required facts before
the national deadline. Depending on the scheme, this may include commitments concerning
crop choice, soil cover, biodiversity, grazing, pesticide use, animal welfare, or other
environmental and agricultural practices.

### Step 4: Administrative and automated checks are performed

The paying agency checks matters such as:

- duplicate claims for the same land;
- whether the declared area overlaps the reference parcel;
- applicant and parcel eligibility;
- consistency with registers and previous declarations;
- whether intervention conditions are satisfied; and
- whether conditionality or other legal requirements affect payment.

Errors may lead to correction, an evidence request, a reduction, or—in serious
cases—an administrative penalty. Intent, severity, recurrence, and the applicable
legal rule matter; not every discrepancy is fraud.

### Step 5: The Area Monitoring System observes the land

The **Area Monitoring System (AMS)** is part of IACS. It uses Copernicus Sentinel data
or other data of at least equivalent value to observe agricultural activities and
conditions over time. Examples include distinguishing cultivated from uncultivated
land, identifying crop development, or detecting mowing and harvesting signals.

Satellite monitoring does not prove every eligibility condition. Cloud cover, parcel
size, mixed land use, ambiguous signals, or a condition that is not observable from
space can create an inconclusive result. The process may then require another data
source, a geo-tagged photograph, a document, or a physical inspection.

### Step 6: Discrepancies are resolved

Where the observed evidence does not match the declaration—or is inconclusive—the
farmer should receive a comprehensible explanation and an opportunity to correct the
claim or provide evidence, according to the applicable rules and deadlines.

This step is essential to procedural fairness. A classification produced by a model or
satellite-processing chain is evidence used by the administration; it should not be
treated as an infallible legal decision.

### Step 7: Payment is calculated and decided

The accredited paying agency determines the eligible area or activity, applies the
relevant unit amounts, reductions, and penalties, and issues the payment decision.
Paying agencies keep the supporting records and report expenditure through the CAP
financial-management system.

### Step 8: Review, appeal, and audit

The route for complaints and appeals is governed mainly by national administrative law
and procedure, within the requirements of EU law. Separately, certification bodies,
the European Commission, and the European Court of Auditors provide assurance over CAP
expenditure. Weak national control systems or ineligible expenditure can lead to EU
financial corrections charged to the Member State rather than to the EU budget.

## 3. Equality is not one question

CAP should be assessed across several dimensions:

| Dimension | Equality question |
|---|---|
| Formal equality | Are applicants subject to clear and consistent rules? |
| Payment equality | Do comparable hectares or activities receive comparable support? |
| Distributional equality | Does support reach those with the greatest income need? |
| Procedural equality | Can all farmers understand, submit, correct, and contest a claim? |
| Geographic equality | Are farmers in different regions or Member States treated comparably? |
| Competitive equality | Do public payments alter competition between supported and unsupported producers? |
| Outcome equality | Do different groups achieve comparable income, resilience, and market access? |

The same policy can improve one dimension and worsen another. A uniform payment per
hectare, for example, looks equal at parcel level but gives a much larger total payment
to a farm controlling more land.

## 4. How CAP can create greater equality within the EU

### Common minimum framework

EU legislation, Commission approval of national plans, common indicators, IACS, audit
requirements, and transparency rules provide a shared baseline. These mechanisms limit
the extent to which national governments can design completely unrelated agricultural
support systems.

### Income stabilisation

Direct payments can reduce income volatility and support continuity of food production,
especially when agricultural income is exposed to weather, disease, and price shocks.
This can narrow the difference between agricultural and non-agricultural earning
conditions, although the effect varies by farm type.

### Redistributive and targeted instruments

The 2023–2027 framework includes instruments intended to improve distribution, such as
complementary redistributive income support, support for young farmers, payments for
areas facing natural constraints, and rural-development interventions. Member States
choose the detailed design within the EU framework.

### Internal and external convergence

- **Internal convergence** reduces differences in payment entitlements within a Member
  State.
- **External convergence** narrows differences in average direct-payment levels between
  Member States.

These mechanisms move the system toward greater payment comparability, but they do not
make payment rates, production costs, farm structures, or national implementation
identical.

### Support for public goods

Eco-schemes and environmental interventions can compensate farmers for practices whose
benefits—such as biodiversity, soil protection, or lower emissions—are not fully priced
by the market. In principle, this prevents farmers who provide public goods from bearing
the entire cost alone.

## 5. How CAP can create or preserve inequality within a country

### Area-based payments favour control of land

Much income support is calculated by eligible hectares. Larger farms therefore tend to
receive larger total payments. The concentration of support partly reflects the
concentration of agricultural land, but it means that equal rates per hectare do not
produce equal support per farmer or support based purely on income need.

Area-based support may also be reflected in rents and land values. Where that happens,
some benefit can flow to landowners rather than remaining entirely with the active
farmer.

### Different farms have different capacity to comply

Complex applications, mapping, record-keeping, evidence collection, and digital tools
create fixed costs. A large farm can spread specialist staff and technology costs over
more hectares; a small farm may rely on an adviser or spend a disproportionate amount
of time on administration.

Digital-only processes can disadvantage applicants with limited connectivity, digital
skills, language support, accessible technology, or access to smartphones capable of
capturing acceptable geo-tagged evidence.

### Monitoring technology does not perform equally everywhere

Satellite and model performance can differ with parcel size, landscape fragmentation,
crop type, tree cover, terrain, weather, and farming practice. If uncertainty is not
handled explicitly, farmers in harder-to-observe landscapes can receive more evidence
requests or inspections even when they comply.

### Historical and structural differences persist

Differences in historic entitlements, land concentration, regional productivity, access
to capital, and bargaining power do not disappear when a common application system is
introduced. CAP can soften these differences, but some payment designs may also
capitalise them.

### National choices affect distribution

Member States choose among permitted interventions and define many detailed conditions
and unit amounts in their Strategic Plans. Choices about redistributive payments,
eco-schemes, coupled support, sector priorities, and national co-financing create
different winners and losers within the common framework.

## 6. Equality and inequality between EU Member States

CAP creates a common market context, but not identical farming conditions.

### Sources of greater equality

- EU financing allows all Member States to operate within a shared agricultural-policy
  framework.
- External convergence reduces some historic differences in direct-payment levels.
- Common control and assurance requirements protect the EU budget and create a minimum
  standard for claim administration.
- Shared environmental objectives reduce the risk that one country gains an advantage
  solely by imposing no comparable obligations.

### Sources of continuing inequality

- Average payments per hectare and the mix of interventions still differ.
- National and regional production costs, wages, land prices, climate, infrastructure,
  and farm structures differ substantially.
- Member States have different administrative capacity, data quality, advisory
  services, and ability to implement digital monitoring.
- Rural-development measures often require national co-financing, so fiscal capacity
  can influence what is delivered.
- Flexibility in Strategic Plans can produce rules with different compliance costs for
  farmers competing in the same EU market.

This is a central CAP trade-off: **subsidiarity** lets countries adapt support to local
needs, while too much divergence can weaken equal treatment and the level playing field.

## 7. EU farmers compared with non-EU farmers

### The basic asymmetry

Only eligible beneficiaries covered by EU Member State CAP systems receive CAP
payments. Farmers in non-EU countries do not receive CAP support merely because they
sell into the EU. Candidate countries may receive separate pre-accession assistance,
but they do not participate fully in CAP until accession and completion of the required
administrative arrangements.

EU farmers receiving support must also meet CAP eligibility, conditionality, control,
and evidence requirements. The comparison is therefore not simply “subsidised EU farmer
versus unregulated foreign farmer”: non-EU producers operate under their own domestic
support systems and must meet applicable EU import, food-safety, plant-health, animal-
health, and product rules when accessing the EU market.

### Ways CAP may advantage EU producers

- Income support can improve liquidity and resilience during low-price or poor-weather
  periods.
- Some market and coupled-support measures can influence production decisions more
  directly than decoupled income support.
- Publicly funded rural infrastructure, investment support, advice, and risk measures
  can improve productivity or reduce business risk.
- Support can enable compliance with standards whose costs would otherwise fall fully
  on the producer.

These channels can make it harder for an otherwise comparable unsupported producer to
compete. The effect depends on the commodity, payment design, market conditions, trade
barriers, exchange rates, and support offered by the non-EU producer's own government.

### Ways CAP obligations may reduce that advantage

- Conditionality and eco-scheme commitments can impose costs or restrict production
  choices.
- Environmental, welfare, traceability, and administrative requirements can be more
  demanding than those faced in some exporting countries.
- Decoupled payments are designed to have less influence on current production than
  price support or payments tied directly to output.

### International trade rules

The World Trade Organization's Agreement on Agriculture distinguishes forms of domestic
support by their expected trade-distorting effect:

- **Amber box** support is considered trade-distorting and is subject to limits and
  reduction commitments, apart from permitted de minimis amounts.
- **Blue box** support is linked to production-limiting programmes and is exempt from
  ordinary amber-box reduction commitments.
- **Green box** support must meet criteria intended to ensure no, or at most minimal,
  trade distortion and is not subject to expenditure limits under the Agreement.

CAP reforms have moved much support away from intervention prices and production-linked
support toward decoupled and environmental measures. This reduces, but does not make
irrelevant, the question of effects on non-EU producers. The EU still uses some coupled
support and market measures, and trade effects must be assessed by instrument and
sector rather than by labelling all CAP expenditure equally distorting.

### Effects on lower-income and developing countries

Possible effects run in more than one direction:

- Subsidised or protected EU production may reduce market opportunities for competing
  exporters or contribute to lower prices in particular markets.
- Lower food prices can benefit net food-importing countries and consumers while
  harming producers of the same product.
- Preferential trade access, development programmes, and demand from the EU can create
  export opportunities, but compliance and certification costs may exclude smaller
  producers.
- A country can contain both winners and losers: consumers, importers, exporters, and
  subsistence farmers do not experience the same effect.

For that reason, claims that CAP simply “helps” or “harms” non-EU countries are too
broad. Analysis should identify the product, support instrument, trade relationship,
and affected group.

## 8. Equality risks in an AI-assisted claim process

The proposed case-study system can improve equality if it:

- checks all parcels consistently instead of relying only on a small inspection sample;
- warns farmers about correctable errors before payment;
- explains the evidence and rule behind a discrepancy;
- provides accessible, multilingual, and assisted channels;
- measures error rates by region, parcel size, crop, and applicant group;
- treats an inconclusive observation as uncertainty rather than non-compliance; and
- preserves meaningful human review and appeal.

It can worsen inequality if it:

- automates historic bias or poor-quality parcel data;
- performs worse on small, fragmented, or unusual parcels;
- places the burden of model uncertainty on the farmer;
- assumes every applicant has equal digital access;
- prioritises inspections using opaque proxies correlated with geography or farm size;
- gives wealthier applicants better access to advisers and counter-evidence; or
- makes an automated classification practically impossible to challenge.

The system should therefore be evaluated not only for aggregate accuracy, but also for
**false-positive and inconclusive rates across relevant farmer, parcel, crop, and
regional groups**.

## 9. Practical fairness tests

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Comparable-case test | Do materially similar claims receive similar decisions? |
| Error-burden test | Which groups receive the most false flags and evidence requests? |
| Accessibility test | Can applicants complete the process without specialist technology or paid advice? |
| Explanation test | Can a farmer understand which rule and evidence produced the result? |
| Correction test | Can an honest mistake be corrected before it becomes a penalty? |
| Appeal test | Is human review timely, independent, and able to change the outcome? |
| Distribution test | Who receives the money: active farmers, large land controllers, tenants, or landowners? |
| Cross-border test | Do national implementation differences create materially different costs for competing EU farmers? |
| Trade test | Does an intervention change production or prices in a way that disadvantages unsupported producers? |

## 10. Relevance to Case Study 47

The case is not only about detecting whether a parcel matches a claim. It concerns the
legitimacy of a public-benefit decision at scale. A successful design must balance:

- protection of EU funds;
- timely and consistent decisions;
- the farmer's right to understand and contest a decision;
- different technical and administrative capabilities among farmers;
- model performance across Hungarian landscapes and farm structures; and
- Commission-level assurance that national expenditure is legal and controlled.

Full-coverage monitoring may reduce arbitrary differences caused by small inspection
samples. It does not automatically create fairness. Fairness depends on how uncertainty,
evidence requests, corrections, penalties, human review, and appeals are designed.

## Interpretation note

This is a policy and process overview, not legal advice. Scheme rules and appeal routes
must be checked against the applicable Member State's current Strategic Plan, national
legislation, annual guidance, and the facts of the individual claim.
